Classification Methodology

OIICS version 3.0 classifies each case across four structures. My process is to read the record fully, identify the injuring event before the outcome, code each structure in order, and document the rationale so the result is consistent and reviewable.

NatureThe physical characteristic of the injury or illness (for example, fracture, amputation).
Part of bodyThe body part directly affected.
Event or exposureThe manner in which the injury or illness was produced.
Source & secondary sourceThe object, substance, or exposure that directly produced or contributed to the injury.

Framework: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Injury and Illness Classification System (OIICS) manual, version 3.0, implemented for 2023 data forward. bls.gov/iif/definitions

Case Classification Samples

Each case applies OIICS event-or-exposure coding to a documented incident type. The event categories and codes are drawn from the BLS OIICS structure as published in the CFOI 2024 results (Table A-9).

Case 1 — Struck by falling object

Incident narrative: A worker on a goods-producing site was struck by an object that fell from above during handling, resulting in a fatal injury.

Event or exposureStruck by falling object
OIICS event code643
Parent categoryContact incidents (6) → Struck by propelled, falling, or suspended object (64)

Rationale: The injuring event is the falling object making contact, so it codes to 643 under the Contact incidents structure, not to a fall by the worker.

Code and category: BLS CFOI 2024, Table A-9. Table A-9, 2024

Case 2 — Caught by running powered equipment

Incident narrative: A maintenance worker was cleaning powered equipment that had not been fully de-energized; the worker was caught by a moving part.

Event or exposureStruck by running powered equipment during maintenance, cleaning, testing
OIICS event code652
Parent categoryContact incidents (6) → Struck, caught, or compressed by running powered equipment (65)

Rationale: The maintenance/cleaning context is captured by the specific subcode 652 within the running-powered-equipment family.

Code and category: BLS CFOI 2024, Table A-9. Table A-9, 2024

Case 3 — Roadway collision, oncoming

Incident narrative: A driver was fatally injured in a roadway collision with another vehicle while moving in opposite directions.

Event or exposureRoadway collision with other vehicle(s), moving in opposite directions, oncoming
OIICS event code2613
Parent categoryTransportation incidents (2) → Roadway incidents (26) → Collision with other vehicle(s) (261)

Rationale: Coded to the most specific level the record supports; the oncoming-direction detail selects subcode 2613.

Code and category: BLS CFOI 2024, Table A-9. Table A-9, 2024

Observations

Transportation incidents were the most frequent fatal event in 2024, accounting for 38.2 percent of all occupational fatalities. The overall decline in fatalities was driven largely by a 16.2 percent drop in deaths from exposure to harmful substances or environments (to 687 from 820), itself driven by fewer drug and alcohol overdoses (410, down from 512). On the nonfatal side, 2.5 million private-industry cases were the lowest in the series going back to 2003, with the total recordable case rate at 2.3 per 100 FTE workers.

Source: BLS SOII 2024 (released Jan 22, 2026). bls.gov/iif

Severe injuries reported to federal OSHA

OSHA's Severe Injury Reports program (employer-reported amputations, hospitalizations, and losses of an eye under 29 CFR 1904.39) recorded 104,585 total reports from January 2015 through September 2025. The most frequent coded event was caught in running equipment or machinery during regular operation (6,685), followed by other fall to lower level (5,913) and caught in running equipment during maintenance/cleaning (5,269), reinforcing the machinery and fall hazards seen in the fatal data.

Source: OSHA Severe Injury Dashboard (data 1/1/2015–9/30/2025). osha.gov/severe-injury-reports

Approach to Ambiguity & Quality

Accurate classification depends on consistent judgment when a record is incomplete. My practice is to code what the record clearly supports, code to the most specific level the evidence allows, document any assumption I make, and flag open questions for a senior analyst rather than guess silently. This keeps the dataset consistent and keeps uncertainty visible.

Example: choosing specificity

OIICS codes are hierarchical. A roadway collision can be coded broadly (Transportation incidents, 2) or specifically (oncoming collision, 2613). When a narrative does not state direction, I code to the parent level the record supports (261) and note what additional detail would be needed to assign the subcode, rather than assuming it. Coding only what the evidence supports protects the integrity of the trend data those codes feed.

On process improvement: documenting coding decisions as I go makes patterns easy to review, supports consistency across a team, and creates a record that helps refine guidance over time.
